\title{\vspace{-1.0cm}\large\bfseries Quantum mesh partitioning for heterogeneous devices:\\
unequal work shares expose a structural limit of a varQITE ansatz\vspace{-0.2cm}}
\author{\normalsize Oussama Turki and Tushar Pandey (Team ZollerQu) --- Qollab $\times$ IonQ Hackathon, October 2026}
\date{\vspace{-0.7cm}}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\noindent\textbf{Abstract.} Quantum graph partitioning has so far been studied for \emph{balanced} bipartitions, notably by IonQ with Ansys and Synopsys inside LS-DYNA. Modern clusters mix CPUs and GPUs, so a mesh must be split with an \emph{unequal} target share $\alpha$ proportional to device speed. We formulate this heterogeneous split as an Ising problem and benchmark QAOA, its variants and IonQ's variational imaginary-time evolution (varQITE) against classical heuristics on nine reproducible finite-element meshes, grading every method against the exact optimum and counting every evaluation. The heterogeneous case changes the picture: varQITE with the published pair-rotation ansatz returns a near-optimal split on $\VQZYequal\%$ of shots for equal devices but fails completely for $\alpha{=}0.8$. We prove why: a qubit that only ever acts as the control of the ansatz's two-qubit gates keeps a 50/50 marginal for all angles, which caps the probability of a unique optimum at $1/2$; equal shares hide this because their optimum is doubly degenerate. QAOA beats random sampling at both shares and matches local search at 12 qubits, but falls behind classical heuristics from 16 qubits on. At 29 qubits ($\QnStates$ splits, simulated on HPC and graded against the exact optimum), the best QAOA settings found at 20 qubits still beat uniform guessing per shot by $\QnGainUniformEq\times$ / $\QnGainUniformCpu\times$ ($\alpha{=}0.5$ / $0.8$). At $\alpha{=}0.8$, however, they reach only $\QnGainShareCpu\times$ the per-shot success of a biased coin that sends each region to the GPU with probability $\alpha$. 1\,000 shots contain a near-optimal split with probability $\QnBestHitEq\%$ / $\QnBestHitCpu\%$, against $\QnSimulatedHitEq\%$ / $\QnSimulatedHitCpu\%$ for simulated annealing with the same budget and $\QnSpectralHitEq\%$ / $\QnSpectralHitCpu\%$ for spectral bisection. Tuning depth and all angles on separate calibration meshes changes this in noiseless simulation: frozen at 29 qubits, $p{=}8$ finds a near-optimal split within 1\,000 shots in $\TuneTwentyNineQaoaEq\%$ / $\TuneTwentyNineQaoaCpu\%$ of the instances (spectral bisection $\TuneTwentyNineSpecEq\%$ / $\TuneTwentyNineSpecCpu\%$), with \TuneTwentyNineGates\ two-qubit gates. On the IonQ Forte~1 noise model (Qollab), varQITE returns the exact optimum in $\IonQPopt\%$ of 1\,000 shots at 12 qubits ($\IonQPoptSecond\%$ in a second run; noiseless $\IonQNoiseless\%$). No quantum advantage is claimed.

Parallel PDE solvers partition their mesh among compute devices; the partition sets both the load balance and the communication volume. IonQ inserted quantum graph partitioning into Ansys/Synopsys LS-DYNA to reduce fill-in of sparse direct solvers, first with varQITE \cite{aboumrad2025,morris2024} and later with Iterative-QAOA \cite{zhu2026}; quantum annealing has been tried for HPC load balancing \cite{qaLB2024}. These studies use balanced halves and closed code. Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Heterogeneous partitioning}: a two-device split with target share $\alpha=s_B/(s_A+s_B)$ from device speeds, plus a device-choice layer for a synthetic multi-device pool.
\item \textbf{A structural limit of the varQITE ansatz under unequal shares} (Lemma~1), measured on all cases, and a one-layer change (A$^+$) that removes the cap.
\item \textbf{An open benchmark graded against the exact optimum}: nine meshes, five QAOA families, varQITE and five classical baselines, with total evaluations counted for every method and all tuning done on held-out meshes; the best settings are re-tested at 29 qubits on HPC, still against the exact optimum.
\item \textbf{Reproducibility}: meshes and region assignments shipped as data files; 30 injected bugs all caught by the checks; one batched IonQ job prepared.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem and model}
\textbf{Benchmark.} Nine plates meshed by Delaunay triangulation (\TriMin--\TriMax\ triangles, Fig.~\ref{fig:objects}; regions in Fig.~\ref{fig:regions}): a rectangle with a known answer, an L-plate with a heat source, two sources, a plate with a hole (stress-style mesh), and a five-step complexity ladder on one L-plate (uniform $\to$ three sources $\to$ heavy-material zone, $3\times$ work $\to$ strong-coupling zone, $3\times$ border cost $\to$ priority zone). Each mesh is grouped into $n$ connected regions (one qubit each) by $k$-means with a connectivity repair; region work $a_i$ and border cost $c_{ij}$ are exact sums. Meshes and region assignments are stored as data files, because floating-point differences made \MeshDiffer\ of \MeshTotal\ meshes differ between macOS/arm64 and Linux/x86-64 builds.

\textbf{Ising model.} With $z_i{=}{+}1$ (device A) or ${-}1$ (device B) and normalised $\tilde a_i$, $\tilde c_{ij}$,
\begin{equation*}
E(z)=\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}\tilde c_{ij}\tfrac{1-z_iz_j}{2}+\lambda\Big(\sum_i\tilde a_iz_i-(1-2\alpha)\Big)^{2}\;[+\mu P(z)],
\end{equation*}
the Ising form of graph partitioning \cite{lucas2014} with a target share. For $\alpha{\neq}0.5$ the balance term contains single-qubit fields $h_i\propto(1-2\alpha)\tilde a_i$, so $E(z)\neq E(-z)$ and the optimum is generically unique. The balance term couples every pair of regions ($n(n{-}1)/2$ two-qubit terms), which suits IonQ's all-to-all connectivity. Scenarios: equal devices ($\alpha{=}0.5$, $\lambda{=}10$) and CPU+GPU with an assumed $4\times$ faster GPU ($\alpha{=}0.8$, $\lambda{=}3.98$); $\lambda$ is shared per scenario and validated by exact enumeration.

\textbf{Device choice (modelled).} For a synthetic pool of \PoolDevices\ devices with assumed speed, memory and power, exhaustive search gives a fastest plan of \PoolFastTime\,h / \PoolFastEnergy\,kWh, an overnight plan ($\le$\PoolNightLimit\,h) of \PoolNightTime\,h / \PoolNightEnergy\,kWh and a lowest-energy plan of \PoolEcoTime\,h / \PoolEcoEnergy\,kWh, against \PoolNaiveTime\,h / \PoolNaiveEnergy\,kWh for equal shares.



\section{Methods}
\textbf{QAOA} (noiseless statevector, cross-checked against Qiskit circuits to $10^{-15}$): cost layer $e^{-i\gamma E}$, mixer $e^{+i\beta\sum X}$; linear-ramp LR-QAOA \cite{lrqaoa2025}; optimised angles with COBYLA, Nelder--Mead, Powell, L-BFGS-B (budget 200 evaluations) and shot-based SPSA \cite{spall1998}; CVaR-QAOA \cite{cvar2020}; warm starts \cite{egger2021}; a symmetry-reduced variant at $\alpha{=}0.5$.
\textbf{varQITE} \cite{morris2024}: each step solves $G\,\dot\theta=d$ by least squares with $d_k={-}(\langle P_kH\rangle-\langle P_k\rangle\langle H\rangle)$ and $G_{kj}=\partial\langle P_k\rangle/\partial\theta_j$ (parameter shift), starting from $|{+}\rangle^{\otimes n}$ with all angles zero. Ansätze: the paper's $e^{i\theta Z_rY_q}$ on all pairs ordered by vertex weight (ZY); $R_Y$ on the target followed by CX on all pairs or on graph edges (A); A with one $R_Y$ per qubit in front (A$^+$, ours); an IonQ-native RZZ + RX ansatz (B). Training uses exact expectations.
\textbf{Classical}: uniform and target-share random sampling, simulated annealing, local search with restarts, spectral bisection.
\textbf{Metrics}: the probability per shot of a split with cost $\le1.05\times$ optimum, and the probability that 1\,000 shots or candidates contain one. \textbf{Total evaluations} count everything a method spends, in hardware-equivalent cost evaluations: $(\text{training circuits}+1)\times1\,000$ shots for quantum methods and the number of candidates for classical ones.
\textbf{Honesty measures}: schedules, step sizes and annealing temperatures are tuned on separate calibration meshes; the p=0 control and the target-share sampler are always reported; a crude depolarising estimate $F{=}(1{-}0.005)^{N_{2q}}$ indicates noise sensitivity.

\section{The control-qubit limit of the varQITE ansatz}
\begin{lemma}
Let $U(\theta)$ be a product of gates that each commute with $Z_r$ for a fixed qubit $r$ (single-qubit gates on other qubits, CX with control $r$, $e^{i\theta Z_rY_q}$, any gate diagonal on $r$). Then for every $\theta$ the measured marginal of $r$ on $U(\theta)|{+}\rangle^{\otimes n}$ is exactly $1/2$, so every bitstring $x^\star$ has probability at most $1/2$.
\end{lemma}
\noindent\emph{Proof.} $P(x_r{=}1)=\langle\psi|\tfrac{1-Z_r}{2}|\psi\rangle$ with $\psi=U|{+}\rangle^{\otimes n}$; since $[U,Z_r]=0$ this equals $\langle{+}|\tfrac{1-Z_r}{2}|{+}\rangle=\tfrac12$, and $P(x^\star)\le P(x_r{=}x^\star_r)=\tfrac12$. $\square$

In the ZY ansatz ordered by vertex weight, the heaviest vertex is the $Z$ side of every pair it touches; in ansatz A it is only ever a CX control. Both therefore contain such a qubit, which we verified numerically on all trained circuits. For $\alpha{=}0.5$ without priority, $E(z){=}E({-}z)$: the optimum comes as a pair $x^\star$, $\bar x^\star$, so a 50/50 qubit costs nothing, which is why the original MaxCut benchmarks did not expose it. For $\alpha{=}0.8$ the optimum is unique and the cap binds. Adding one $R_Y$ per qubit (A$^+$) breaks the commutation and removes the cap.

Warm starts inherit most of their success from the spectral split; the convex-relaxation warm start is degenerate here ($c_i{=}\alpha$ for every region).

\textbf{varQITE.} At $\alpha{=}0.5$ the ZY ansatz converges to near-optimal splits ($\VQZYequal\%$ per shot, 30--100\% on every case) with 66 two-qubit gates, at a cost of $\VQZYtotal$ total evaluations. At 16 qubits (graph-edge variants), A$^+$ reaches $r{=}\VQLApEq$ / $\VQLApCpu$ and no variant exceeds $\VQLnearMax\%$ per shot.

\textbf{Set-up.} 29 qubits is the size of IonQ's Forte~1 noise-model simulator. We took the best settings found at 20 qubits and re-tested them, without any further tuning, on cases 2 and 9 split into 29 regions (Table~\ref{tab:q29}). The selection rule was fixed before the run: per scenario, (i) the fixed schedule with the highest mean per-shot success at $n{=}20$ among all LR schedule families and depths $p\le5$; (ii) QAOA+COBYLA at its best depth ($p{=}2$), transferred as the mean of its six optimised angle sets, whose standard deviation across instances is at most \QnAngleSpread\,rad; (iii) LR-QAOA $p{=}2$, for continuity with Table~\ref{tab:scaling}. A dedicated complex64 statevector code builds the energy of every split from two $2^{15}$-state halves and applies the mixer in cache-sized blocks, so one instance fits in 7\,GB. It agrees with the notebook's float64 simulator to $\QnValidationMaxDiff$ or better in every probability at \QnValidationSizes\ qubits. The \QnInstances\ instances ran in parallel on two 32\,GB cloud servers (about \QnInstanceMinutes\ minutes each). Because every energy is computed anyway, every method, classical ones included, is graded against the exact optimum, unlike the 24-qubit column of Table~\ref{tab:scaling}.

\textbf{Result.} The best fixed schedule puts $\QnBestShotEq$ / $\QnBestShotCpu$ of its probability on near-optimal splits ($\alpha{=}0.5$ / $0.8$). That is $\QnGainUniformEq\times$ / $\QnGainUniformCpu\times$ uniform guessing, so the settings transfer and QAOA still concentrates probability on good splits. At $\alpha{=}0.8$, though, most of that gain is the work share itself: a biased coin that sends each region to the GPU with probability $\alpha$ does better per shot (QAOA reaches $\QnGainShareCpu\times$ its success), in line with the 12-qubit ablation where the single-region terms carried most of the effect. In absolute terms QAOA is far behind: 1\,000 shots contain a near-optimal split with probability $\QnBestHitEq\%$ / $\QnBestHitCpu\%$. With the same 1\,000 evaluations, simulated annealing succeeds in $\QnSimulatedHitEq\%$ / $\QnSimulatedHitCpu\%$ of its runs and local search in $\QnLocalHitEq\%$ / $\QnLocalHitCpu\%$. Spectral bisection (56 evaluations) is optimal on $\QnSpectralHitEq\%$ / $\QnSpectralHitCpu\%$ of the instances. The best of 1\,000 QAOA shots is on average as far from the optimum as the annealing result at $\alpha{=}0.5$ and much further at $\alpha{=}0.8$ (gap columns). The noise estimate removes even the per-shot gain on hardware: $F{=}0.995^{2030}{\approx}\QnFidBestEq$ for $p{=}5$, so the circuit would sample essentially uniformly. varQITE was not run at this size: one training step of the ZY ansatz needs about $2\times406$ parameter-shifted simulations of $2^{29}$ amplitudes (estimated at days per step on these servers).

\section{Tuning QAOA itself}
Same Hamiltonian, $\lambda$ and test meshes; only the QAOA settings change. Depth $p\in\{1,\dots,6,8\}$, the LR schedule, all $2p$ angles (COBYLA, Nelder--Mead, Powell, L-BFGS-B, SPSA, equal budgets) and warm starts were chosen on 18 calibration meshes (all nine cases, seeds 101/102, never scored), frozen, and scored once on the test meshes; the Task~04 set of four calibration meshes over-fitted at $p{=}3$--4. \section{Discussion and limitations}
The heterogeneous share is not a cosmetic generalisation: it breaks the spin-flip symmetry that balanced benchmarks enjoy, and with it the success of a published ansatz. QAOA, whose mixer acts on every qubit, does not have this blind spot. With the earlier settings it was outpaced by classical heuristics beyond 12 qubits; with depth and angles tuned on calibration meshes it matches or exceeds them in noiseless simulation up to 29 qubits, at a gate count no current noisy device would survive. All results are noiseless simulations or models; at 29 qubits the angles are transferred, not re-optimised; varQITE training used exact expectations; device speeds, runtimes and energies are assumptions. Next steps: run the other prepared circuits (and the CPU+GPU scenario) on the IonQ noise model; build single-qubit layers like A$^+$ into deeper varQITE ansätze; recursive splits for more than two devices; measured CPU/GPU runs on refined meshes.
